AbstractSmall satellites must perform precise, fuel-limited maneuvers under strict constraints. Model Predictive Control (MPC) is attractive as it can plan future actions while enforcing these limitations. This work addresses two questions: (1) whether MPC can run in real time with binary (on/off), asymmetric thrusters, and (2) whether a high-fidelity "digital twin" can be used to tune and validate the controller before hardware testing.This thesis validates an MPC framework on a planar satellite prototype. The platform floats on air bearings, is commanded at 16.7 Hz (60 ms period), and uses eight binary thrusters individually characterized with up to 13.2% force variation. A mixed-integer MPC solves for optimal on/off thruster sequences to minimize tracking error and propellant use. A corresponding digital twin was built using shared physical parameters. The same controller was used to execute a complex mission in both simulation and hardware.Experimental results show close agreement. Over a ≈200 s mission, the hardware achieved a mean position tracking error of 0.179 m, a final position error of 0.024 m, and a final attitude error of 0.51°. Total thruster-on time, a proxy for propellant use, differed by only 1.5%. The MPC solver met every 60 ms deadline, with an average solve time of 4.61 ms and a worst-case of 32.10 ms.These results demonstrate that: (1) mixed-integer MPC with binary thrusters is computationally feasible for this problem, and (2) the digital twin is sufficiently predictive to tune controller weights in simulation and execute them on hardware with minimal errors. This thesis provides an experimentally supported development process linking simulation, controller design, and hardware execution.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION1.1 MotivationEarth orbit is now populated by many thousands of active satellites, with large constellations and inspection-class proximity operations becoming normal rather than exceptional. Recent analyses estimate more than 10,000 active satellites on orbit, with hundreds to thousands more added each year as commercial, civil, and defense operators expand autonomous capability in low Earth orbit.As satellite density increases, close-range maneuvers (inspection, servicing, formation keeping, and cooperative docking) demand precise relative motion control under tight resource limits. This is especially true for nanosatellites and CubeSats, which typically operate with very limited propellant mass, low-power computing, and strict actuator constraints [2], [4]. Small satellites often rely on low-thrust cold-gas or electric microthrusters, sometimes commanded only in on/off pulses rather than smoothly throttled levels [2]. These hardware constraints define a hard problem: maintain accurate position and attitude tracking around a target while minimizing propellant and staying inside strict safety limits.Classical control schemes (e.g., PID, or LQR with saturation clamps) can stabilize a satellite, but they struggle once you demand: hard state limits (don’t exceed a velocity cap, don’t rotate faster than X), strict keep-out constraints near structure, explicit propellant minimization, and binary thruster decisions instead of continuous thrust.Model Predictive Control (MPC) is well-suited to this setting. MPC works by predicting future system behavior over a short horizon, then solving an optimization problem to choose the best control action at each update. Because it solves a constrained optimal control problem directly, MPC can enforce safety constraints and trade off accuracy versus fuel use in a mathematically explicit way. This capability has made MPC increasingly attractive in satellite guidance and attitude control research, including recent formulations that integrate both translational and rotational control, tolerate actuator faults, and explicitly respect actuator limits [2], [3].But two practical barriers remain for small satellites and proximity operations: Trust: Mission developers will not rely on a guidance/control method unless they can predict how it will behave on real hardware. Runtime feasibility: MPC for binary thrusters is a mixed-integer optimization problem. Solving a mixed-integer program every 50–60 ms sounds infeasible for flight hardware unless you can prove that it actually runs in real time.Meanwhile, the space industry is moving toward model-based engineering and “digital continuity” keeping a single source of truth that flows from design to simulation to test, specifically to drive down cost and schedule. European Space Agency planning documents describe digital continuity and model-based systems engineering as central to reducing program cost and shortening timelines in future satellite programs. This motivates the core question of this work: can we rely on a high-fidelity digital twin, plus an MPC controller that reasons over real hardware limits, to predict and then reproduce precise, resource-efficient planar satellite like motion on physical hardware?1.2 Research ObjectiveThe central objective of this thesis is to determine whether a digital twin can be used not just for visualization, but as a reliable predictor of how an MPC-controlled satellite prototype will behave in real testing environment.Concretely, the thesis addresses three questions: Simulation-to-Hardware Fidelity:Can a physics-based digital twin predict position tracking accuracy, heading behavior, propellant resource usage, and final settling accuracy (position and yaw) closely enough that controller tuning can be done in simulation before hardware testing? Algorithmic Compensation for Hardware Imperfections:The eight thrusters on the physical platform are commercial on/off solenoid valves with no guarantee of matching performance, measured steady state thrust levels differ by more than 10% across the set. Instead of buying tightly matched valves, this work characterizes each thruster individually and feeds those measured thrust values directly into both the MPC controller and the simulation. Does that allow the controller to compensate for actuator asymmetry in practice, or does hardware mismatch still dominate? Real-Time Feasibility of Mixed-Integer MPC:The controller explicitly treats each thruster command as a binary decision variable (on/off) at every control step and solves a mixed-integer optimization problem at 16.7 Hz (one solves every 60 ms). Can such a controller meet all real-time deadlines for a full, minutes-long mission without missing an update?The rest of the thesis is structured to answer these three questions experimentally.1.3 Approach: Digital Twin Validation MethodologyThe methodology couples a high-fidelity simulation environment (“digital twin”) and a physical planar satellite prototype and then measures how closely they agree under the same mission, with the same controller, and the same parameters.Step 1: Shared controller and shared parameters.The same Model Predictive Controller is used in both simulation and hardware. The prediction horizon (15 steps), control horizon (12 steps), time step (60 ms per control action), cost weights, state constraints, and solver configuration are identical. The simulation and the hardware also use the same physical parameters: total mass and inertia, the measured center of mass offset, the measured thrust level for each individual thruster, damping terms and actuator timing delay.Step 2: Mission replay in both environments.A single mission (“Profile Following”) is executed in simulation and then on hardware. The mission includes curved translation, heading changes, straight segments, and final settling at a target pose with centimeter-level accuracy. State histories, thruster commands, and solver timing are logged in both cases.Step 3: Quantitative comparison.The simulation output and hardware data are compared using the same metrics: Planar position tracking error over time and at the final target, Heading (yaw) tracking error over time and final heading accuracy, Velocity profile, Total usage per thruster, MPC solve time per control step.This produces not just a qualitative “it looked similar,” but statistical error comparisons (mean, max, standard deviation, final values) between hardware and simulation. That is how we assess digital twin fidelity and real-time MPC feasibility.This approach mirrors the model-based, digitally driven workflow being promoted in current satellite development practice, where high-fidelity models are used to reduce on-hardware iteration cost.1.4 Experimental PlatformThe physical system is a planar satellite prototype. A cubic 290 mm ? 290 mm ? 150 mm body is supported by three air bearings that float on an epoxy floor. The air bearings create a near-frictionless air film, constraining the satellite to x–y translation and yaw rotation in a single plane while approximating planar microgravity conditions.Actuation is provided by eight independently addressable compressed-air thrusters (commercial 12 V solenoid valves operated as binary on/off devices). These thrusters are mounted in symmetric pairs around the perimeter, allowing both translation and controlled yaw torque. Each valve was experimentally characterized in situ, measured steady state thrust magnitudes varied by roughly 13% across the set. Those measured values are not “trimmed out” in hardware they are built into the controller.Full 2D pose (x, y, heading) is tracked by a seven-camera OptiTrack motion capture system running at 240 Hz. The motion capture data is streamed to a ground station computer, which solves the MPC problem every 60 ms. The optimal 8-bit thruster command (one bit per valve) is then transmitted wirelessly to an onboard ESP32-S3 microcontroller, which energizes the appropriate valves through relay drivers. This division of labor, optimization on the ground station, actuation onboard reflects the reality of resource-limited satellite, where onboard computer is constrained to low-power microcontrollers [2], [4].This integrated setup enables direct, time-aligned comparison between the digital twin and the hardware for the exact same mission.1.5 ContributionsThis thesis makes five primary contributions: Planar satellite prototype with characterized actuation.A modular, air-bearing-supported planar satellite was designed and built. The satellite includes eight perimeter thrusters, wireless actuation, external motion capture for state estimation, and dual-pressure pneumatics for both support bearings and thrusters. Each thruster was individually characterized in its installed configuration. The measured thrust magnitudes differed by more than 10% across the set. Rather than eliminating this asymmetry through hardware selection, this work measures it and uses it. Real-time mixed-integer MPC with binary thrusters.A Model Predictive Controller was implemented that explicitly models each thruster as an on/off decision variable. At every 60 ms control step, the controller solves a mixed-integer optimization problem with the linearized satellite dynamics, actuator constraints, velocity and angular rate limits, and a cost function penalizing tracking error and control effort. The controller runs at 16.7 Hz and successfully computes control commands in real time for the entire mission. A digital twin with shared controller code and shared parameters.A digital twin was developed that mirrors the mass, inertia, center of mass offset, thruster locations, and individually measured thrust magnitudes of the physical platform. The simulation includes damping and actuation timing effects representative of the air-bearing environment. Critically, the exact same MPC implementation (same solver, same horizons, same weights, same constraints) is executed in simulation and on hardware. The simulation is therefore not just “graphical,” it is controller-identical and parameter-identical. Full-mission comparison between simulation and hardware.A multi-minute “Profile Following” mission was executed in both environments with no controller retuning between them. The mission includes curved path segments, heading changes, and a terminal settle. Time histories of position, heading, thruster usage, and solver timing are captured for both runs. Quantified simulation-to-hardware fidelity.The hardware run achieved a mean position error of 0.179 m during tracking and a final position error of 0.024 m at the target. The final heading error was 0.51°. Total thruster usage differed by only about 1.5% between hardware and simulation. The MPC solver met all real-time deadlines in hardware: the average solve time was 4.61 ms, the worst observed solve time was 32.10 ms, and no control update missed the 60 ms period.These results show that (1) the same mixed-integer MPC can control both the digital twin and the hardware without retuning, (2) the digital twin predicts hardware behavior with useful accuracy, and (3) mixed-integer MPC with binary thrusters is feasible in real time for multi-minute missions on a resource-limited planar satellite prototype.Together, these contributions demonstrate a workflow in which controller development and tuning can happen primarily in simulation, and hardware time is used to validate rather than discover basic behaviors exactly the kind of digital-continuity process currently being pushed in modern satellite development practice.  1.6 Thesis OrganizationChapter 2 reviews related work in satellite MPC, hybrid (on/off) thruster control, planar air-bearing and geometric/actuator layout studies.Chapter 2: Literature Review2.1 Model Predictive Control for SatelliteModel Predictive Control (MPC) is now a standard tool in advanced aerospace guidance, navigation, and control. MPC computes a sequence of future control inputs by solving an optimization problem over a prediction horizon, subject to system dynamics and explicit constraints such as actuator limits, angular rate bounds, and position safety regions. Only the first control input from that sequence is applied, the process repeats at the next control step using the updated state. This "receding horizon" structure gives MPC two properties that are especially important for satellite: (1) it can reason about constraints directly, and (2) it can anticipate future behavior instead of reacting only to current error [5].Satellites are a natural application for MPC because they are highly constrained systems. Attitude must remain within limits, body rates must stay below structural and sensor tolerances, and propellant is limited. Classical linear control methods such as PID and LQR can stabilize attitude and reject disturbances, but they do not natively enforce hard constraints. Constraint handling in those methods is usually added afterward using improvised saturation techniques, which can break optimality and, in some cases, can break stability. MPC, in contrast, treats constraints as first-class problem [5].One of the first demonstrations of MPC on satellite hardware-like settings was explicit MPC for satellite attitude control [5]. In explicit MPC, instead of solving a constrained optimization problem online at every control step, the optimal solution is pre-computed offline as a piecewise affine state-feedback law. The result is a lookup-like controller that can be evaluated on embedded hardware with very limited computational resources. Hegrenaes, Gravdahl, and Tøndel showed that explicit MPC could control satellite attitude under both actuator constraints and rate constraints, and that it could be implemented in real time in a way that was compatible with flight-class processors [5]. This work demonstrated that MPC-type logic was not only theoretically appealing but could actually run within realistic onboard timing budgets for satellite control.The ability to pre-compute and store explicit MPC laws was enabled by advances in multi-parametric optimization. The Multi-Parametric Toolbox (MPT), and in particular MPT3, provides algorithms to compute piecewise affine feedback laws and invariant sets for constrained linear systems, and includes code generation for embedded implementations [6]. These tools make it possible to turn a constrained optimal control problem into a real-time controller that is guaranteed to respect actuator limits and state bounds. In other words, the MPC can be compiled down to a deterministic, certified control law that fits on embedded hardware [6]. This line of work established a bridge between high-performance optimal control theory and flight-feasible implementation.More recent MPC research for satellites has focused on robustness. In real systems, the true dynamics deviate from the nominal model due to actuator asymmetry, external disturbances, and parameter drift. Tube-based MPC (TBMPC) addresses this by planning a nominal trajectory using a nominal model and then surrounding that trajectory with an invariant "tube" that guarantees that the disturbed, real trajectory remains bounded inside it [7]. In TBMPC, the optimization problem enforces constraints not on the nominal state directly, but on a tightened version that accounts for the worst-case deviation under bounded disturbances and actuator saturation. Meinert and Baldauf applied tube-based MPC to satellite attitude tracking, showing that the approach can enforce pointing constraints and actuator limits while maintaining robustness to bounded disturbances and modeling errors [7]. The important point is that robustness is handled explicitly in the controller structure not by "adding gain margin," but by keeping the system close to the desired attitude within a defined margin [7].Together, these results show a clear progression in satellite control: Early work established that MPC can be made fast enough for real-time satellite attitude control using explicit solutions [5], [6]. More recent work extends MPC formulations to guarantee robustness through tube-based approaches that explicitly account for bounded disturbances and actuator limitations [7].The testbed in this thesis builds directly on that trajectory. We apply MPC to a fully actuated planar free-flyer with 3-DOF motion (x, y, yaw). The controller is solved online as a mixed-integer program (Chapter 5), rather than as an offline explicit controller, and it is validated both in simulation and on hardware (Chapter 6). Like [5], [6], [7], the controller enforces state and actuator constraints directly in the optimization. Unlike [5], [6], [7], our system uses only on/off thrusters rather than continuously controllable torquers or reaction wheels.2.2 Binary Thrusters and Hybrid ControlNot all satellites have continuously variable thrusters. Cold gas jets, monopropellant valves, and other small satellite actuators are sometimes binary: they are either firing at a roughly fixed thrust level, or they are off. This creates a hybrid control problem. The satellite dynamics are continuous, but the actuators are discrete.This can be handled with pulse-modulation techniques instead of optimization. The controller first computes a desired "continuous" control torque or force, then a secondary layer converts that continuous request into a sequence of thruster pulses. Examples include pulse-width and pulse-frequency modulation (PWPF/PWFM), in which the duty cycle and/or firing frequency of the thruster are modulated so that the time-averaged impulse matches the requested control effort [8], [9]. These techniques are attractive because they are lightweight, they run in real time on small embedded processors, and they have decades of flight heritage. Thurman and Flashner introduced a pulse-modulation technique aimed at guidance and control of automated satellite that shaped the pulse sequence directly to satisfy guidance demands using on/off thrusters [8]. Later work (e.g., Khosravi and Sarhadi) refined PWPF logic and validated it through simulation and hardware-oriented test scenarios, showing that duty-cycle modulation can regulate satellite attitude while honoring minimum impulse bit and valve timing limits [9].While modulation-based approaches are practical, they separate "compute the ideal control input" from "make that input achievable with binary thrusters". That split can make it hard to reason about fuel usage, constraint satisfaction, or coupling between translation and rotation.Modern MPC-based approaches take a different view. Instead of first computing a continuous input and then approximating it with pulses, MPC can directly choose which thrusters fire at each time step. In this formulation, each thruster command is represented by a binary decision variable (1 = fire, 0 = off), and the controller solves an optimization problem that includes those binary variables as part of the decision [7]. That turns the control problem into a mixed-integer program. The benefit is that fuel usage, actuator limits, coupling between rotation and translation, and settling accuracy are handled in a single optimization layer. The cost is computational complexity.There is active research on how to make such hybrid MPC approaches flight feasible. One path uses branch-and-bound MILP solvers and attempts to solve the full mixed-integer problem in real time on the ground or on capable processors. Another path uses heuristics and metaheuristics. For example, Naronglerdrit and Parnichkun proposed a genetic-algorithm (GA)-based MPC to simultaneously control satellite attitude and orbital motion using on/off thrusters [10]. Their approach searches over candidate thruster activation sequences using evolutionary optimization. The method was designed for computational efficiency and robustness to parameter uncertainties and can in principle, be deployed on low-cost embedded processors where deterministic MILP solvers are impractical [10]. This is a fundamentally different trade: GA-based MPC sacrifices deterministic optimality guarantees and consistent solve time in exchange for onboard deployability.The system in this thesis uses the first path. We formulate MPC as a mixed-integer problem, with binary variables representing thruster firing decisions, and we solve it at 16.7 Hz (60 ms period). The optimization explicitly penalizes position error, orientation error, residual velocity, and total thruster usage, while respecting actuator constraints. This puts the binary actuator logic inside the controller instead of adding it on afterward. That structure makes it possible to compare, one-to-one, what the controller predicts in simulation versus what actually happens when the same binary thrusters fire on the physical prototype.In short: Pulse-modulation (PWPF/PWFM) converts continuous control effort into discrete thruster commands and is lightweight to execute [8], [9]. GA-based MPC uses heuristic search to produce discrete thruster sequences for both orbit and attitude regulation, targeting embedded feasibility [10]. Mixed-integer MPC encodes the binary thruster decisions directly into the optimization layer and solves them in real time.2.3 Ground Testbeds, Air-Bearing Platforms, and Digital TwinsBecause on-orbit testing is expensive satellite control algorithms are traditionally validated on the ground using physical simulators. A common approach is a planar air-bearing testbed. The satellite floats on a near-frictionless film of air above a smooth surface, allowing translation in x-y and rotation about the vertical axis. This produces a good approximation of 2D microgravity and is widely used for studying satellite rendezvous, inspection maneuvers, docking, and attitude control logic [11].A modern planar air-bearing testbed typically includes: a floating platform with thrusters or reaction actuators, an air-bearing support assembly that offloads weight and minimizes friction, a metrology system such as motion capture for sub-millimeter state feedback, and a ground computer that runs the controller in real time and commands the test object.Huang et al. describe such a system in detail, including bearing layout, pneumatic regulation, sensing architecture, and characterization procedures for dynamic parameters like mass properties and damping [11]. They emphasize that accurate testbed characterization is critical: if the inertia, center of mass, or residual drag are mis-modeled, controller validation results can be misleading [11]. Careful system identification and calibration are therefore a prerequisite for meaningful controller testing.Beyond planar free-flyers, some groups have developed hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) facilities that couple real satellite hardware to mechanical simulators so that guidance and control algorithms can be stressed under realistic dynamic conditions. Governale et al. describe a HIL setup for in-plane relative motion testing of tethered or multi-body systems using a tip-tilt flat table facility [12]. In that setup, flight-like processors, actuators, and estimation logic run in the loop while a physical mechanism emulates reduced-gravity dynamics and relative motion constraints relevant to on-orbit formation and capture scenarios [12]. Facilities like this allow researchers to test controller robustness in closed loop and observe failure behavior (for example, actuator saturation or estimator divergence) without risks.The software counterpart to these physical testbeds is the digital twin. In this context, a digital twin is a high-fidelity software model of the satellite, its actuators, and the test environment (including residual drag, actuator delays, and sensor noise). A digital twin serves two roles: Software-in-the-loop (SIL) development. The controller is run entirely in simulation. The digital twin predicts how the system will move for a given thruster command sequence, including disturbances and saturation. This accelerates controller tuning because many controller variants can be evaluated quickly without consuming lab time or wearing hardware. Calibration against hardware. After physical tests are run, the logged hardware data (trajectory, attitude, thruster usage, solve times) is compared directly to the digital twin's prediction for the same mission. Discrepancies are used to update the digital twin's parameters e.g., damping coefficients, mass properties, thrust magnitudes until the simulation reproduces the hardware response [11], [12].This thesis uses that exact loop. Chapter 4 documents the digital twin for the planar satellite, including the physics engine, actuator model, and sensing pipeline. Chapter 6 compares the digital twin's predictions to the real testbed execution of the same mission. This is not only used to validate the MPC controller, but also to measure how accurately the digital twin predicts fuel usage, tracking error, heading regulation, and solver timing.2.4 Simulation-to-Hardware Fidelity and Remaining GapsThe long-term goal in satellite control is to design, test, and validate guidance and control laws largely in simulation then execute them in hardware with minimal surprises. The barrier is the sim-to-real gap: differences between how a controller behaves in the digital twin and how it behaves on the real system.For planar air-bearing free-flyers and related HIL setups, several recurring sources of mismatch have been identified: Actuator asymmetry. Small cold-gas or pneumatic thrusters rarely produce identical force levels, and uncalibrated asymmetry can be a significant source of mismatch. Residual drag and unmodeled coupling. Even "frictionless" planar satellites experience frictions are not present in idealized simulation [11]. Timing and communication delays. Real systems incur wireless transmission delay, relay and valve actuation delay, and solver timing jitter. These timing effects alter transient response and settling behavior in ways that are difficult to predict unless they are explicitly modeled. Estimation differences. Simulation can provide perfect state feedback, hardware state estimation is subject to noise, dropout, and discretization limits, even with high-end motion capture [11], [12].Prior work has emphasized the need to characterize these effects and incorporate them back into the simulation model [11], [12]. The general workflow is: run the mission in hardware, measure what actually happened, then update the simulation model so that when the mission is rerun in simulation, it reproduces the measured behavior. Governale et al. argue that high-fidelity HIL is essential for validating not just nominal performance, but also failure modes and off-nominal behaviors that do not naturally appear in ideal simulation [12]. Huang et al. make a similar point for planar air-bearing systems: without validated mass properties, damping, and actuator models, quantitative claims about controller performance are not trustworthy [11].Even with these advances, there are still gaps in the published record: MPC with binary thrusters has largely been reported either in simulation or in limited hardware trials, but published studies rarely quantify the full set of performance metrics that matter for mission design at once (trajectory error, final settling accuracy, fuel usage, and solve-time margins). PWPF-style modulation work shows attitude regulation with on/off thrusters [8], [9], and GA-based MPC has been demonstrated for coupled orbit/attitude control in simulation with heuristic search [10], but there is limited published work that reports closed-loop experimental results from a mixed-integer MPC that directly optimizes binary thruster activations in real time. Digital twins are widely used in aerospace development, and air-bearing/HIL facilities are well established [11], [12], but most prior reports focus either on testbed design or on controller concept demonstration. Fewer studies present a mission-level, side-by-side comparison between simulation and hardware using identical controller code and identical mission definition and then quantify how closely the two match across multiple metrics.This thesis addresses those gaps directly. First, the controller implemented here is a mixed-integer MPC that commands eight binary thrusters on a planar free-flyer, with real-time solution of the optimization problem at 16.7 Hz. The same controller structure, cost function, constraints, horizons, and linearized dynamics model are used in both simulation (digital twin) and hardware. Second, hardware execution and simulation of the exact same mission are compared quantitatively in Chapter 6. Metrics include: trajectory tracking error over time, final position and heading error, velocity regulation, total thruster firing time as a proxy for "fuel," and solve-time statistics. This enables a direct estimate of sim-to-real fidelity for the MPC formulation and the digital twin.2.5 SummaryFour themes emerge from the literature: MPC is well suited to satellite attitude and translational control because it naturally enforces input and state constraints and can plan ahead in time [5], [6]. Robust MPC variants such as tube-based MPC extend this capability to handle bounded disturbances and model uncertainty while maintaining constraint satisfaction [7]. Binary thrusters introduce inherent hybrid behavior. Legacy solutions use pulse-modulation logic to approximate a continuous control law with on/off actuators [8], [9]. More recent work explores evolutionary or heuristic optimization for combined attitude and orbital control using only on/off thrusters [10]. Mixed-integer MPC goes further by embedding the binary firing decisions directly into the optimal control problem and solving it online. That is the approach implemented in this thesis. Ground testbeds such as planar air-bearing platforms and hardware-in-the-loop facilities are the standard path for de-risking satellite controls before flight. High-fidelity characterization of mass properties, damping, sensing, and actuator behavior is essential to make those testbeds predictive [11], [12]. Despite progress, there is still limited published, quantitative evidence that an MPC controller with binary thrusters can (a) run in real time, (b) meet accuracy and timing requirements on physical hardware, and (c) be predicted in advance by a calibrated digital twin.Chapters 3-6 of this thesis are structured to provide that evidence. Chapter 3 describes the physical testbed, including the pneumatic actuation system, the air-bearing support assembly, the motion capture infrastructure, and the communication and power architecture. Chapter 4 describes the digital twin software stack and how it mirrors the physical system. Chapter 5 formulates the mixed-integer MPC used for control. Chapter 6 presents quantitative, mission-level comparisons between simulation and hardware, providing an experimental measure of sim-to-real fidelity for MPC with discrete thrusters.Chapter 3: System Design3.1 System OverviewThis chapter describes the design and implementation of a ground-based planar satellite testbed used to validate the Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework presented in Chapter 5 and experimentally evaluated in Chapter 6. The system is designed to reproduce in-plane satellite like motion in a controlled laboratory setting by floating a cubic satellite platform on three air bearings over an epoxy floor. This configuration allows near-frictionless translation in x–y and rotation about the vertical axis, providing a high-fidelity approximation of planar microgravity. The testbed consists of a 290 mm ? 290 mm ? 150 mm satellite platform actuated by eight binary (on/off) thrusters and supported by an air-bearing support assembly. Seven OptiTrack Prime 13 motion capture cameras provide ground-truth planar position and heading measurements at 240 Hz with sub-millimeter and sub-degree accuracy. A ground station computer solves the mixed-integer MPC problem at 16.7 Hz (60 ms control period) and transmits thruster firing commands wirelessly to an onboard ESP32-S3 microcontroller. The same MPC formulation, solver configuration, and horizons described in Chapter 5 are used in both hardware and simulation and are evaluated in Chapter 6.3.1.1 Design PhilosophyThe system is built around four guiding principles:Commercial off-the-shelf hardware: The platform uses standard pneumatic, structural, and electronic components rather than aerospace-grade hardware.Algorithmic compensation: Instead of matching hardware components to tight tolerances, the system accepts actuator variation and compensates for it through accurate characterization and MPC optimization.Modular construction: The satellite and support hardware are built from aluminum extrusions and 3D-printed PLA parts. Components can be repositioned, replaced or reconfigured with minimal machining.Ground-based MPC execution: The computationally expensive MPC optimization is solved on a ground station computer and only the resulting on/off thruster commands are sent wirelessly to the satellite platform for actuation. This allows high-rate, branch-and-bound MILP control without requiring a flight-class onboard processor.This approach demonstrates that controllability, repeatability, and quantitative evaluation of MPC performance can be achieved using commercially available hardware, provided that the system is accurately modeled and properly characterized.3.2 Mechanical DesignThe mechanical design consists of two primary elements: (1) the satellite platform, which is the controlled body carrying thrusters, electronics, and sensing infrastructure, and (2) the air-bearing support assembly, which provides the low-friction planar test environment. under realistic operating conditions. The objective of the digital twin is to enable controller development, tuning, and mission planning in software, and to generate performance predictions that can be compared directly with real hardware tests. The same Model Predictive Controller (MPC) described in Chapter 5 is executed in both environments.The digital twin consists of two main elements: A planar rigid-body physics engine that models the satellite's translational and rotational motion under thruster actuation, including environmental effects such as drag and small disturbances. A virtual satellite model with the same mass properties, thruster locations, measured thrust magnitudes, and center-of-mass offset as the real hardware.The controller and mission logic run unmodified in both simulation and on the physical platform. This allows a mission to be "flown" in software first and then run on hardware using the same MPC structure, horizons, and tuning.In addition, a complete 3D CAD model of the system was created in Autodesk Fusion 360. The simulation itself is planar (2D), since only in-plane motion is exercised on the epoxy floor.4.2 The Physics Engine4.2.1 Purpose and RequirementsThe physics engine is the core of the digital twin simulation. It advances the satellite's state in time by applying thruster forces and integrating the resulting motion. To be useful in development and validation, the physics engine must: Implement planar rigid-body dynamics (translation in x–y and rotation about the vertical axis).  Correctly transform forces from the body-fixed frame to the world frame. Account for the fact that the center of mass is not perfectly at the geometric center of the square chassis. Support both idealized (frictionless, noise-free) and realistic (damped, disturbed, noisy) environments.4.2.2 State RepresentationThe satellite's motion is represented by a six-dimensional state vector containing position, velocity, orientation, and angular velocity:,x,t.=,,,p-x.,t.-,p-y.,t.-,v-x.,t.-,v-y.,t.-?,t.-?,t...#,4.1..Here: p_x(t), p_y(t) are the x and y position coordinates in the inertial (world) frame [m].  v_x(t), v_y(t) are linear velocities in the inertial frame [m/s].  \theta(t) is the in-plane orientation angle [rad], measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis.  \omega(t) is the angular velocity [rad/s] about the vertical axis. This state fully describes the planar configuration of the satellite at any instant and predicts the next state under known applied forces and torques.Internally, the simulation stores state in the order [p_x,p_y,v_x,v_y,\theta,\omega,]-T.. In Chapter 5, the MPC formulation uses an alternate ordering [p_x,p_y,\theta,v_x,v_y,\omega,]-T. to group position and orientation before velocities. These modules share a common configuration, call the same MPC controller, and produce compatible logs. No module maintains its own private copy of these parameters. This guarantees that the mission flown in simulation and the mission flown on hardware use the same MPC tuning and the same physical assumptions.4.4.2 Model Predictive Controller (MPC.py)MPC.py implements the Model Predictive Controller described in Chapter 5. At each control step it: Accepts the current measured or estimated state of the satellite. Accepts the current reference (desired state) from the mission layer. Uses linearized dynamics for the current orientation and velocity. Solves a mixed-integer optimization problem within a fixed time limit.The output of MPC.py is: An 8-element binary command vector indicating which thrusters should fire during the upcoming control interval. Diagnostic information including solver status and measured solve time.MPC.py also supports warm starts by shifting the previous optimal solution forward in time and using it as an initial guess for the next solve. The MPC interface is identical in simulation and on hardware: both Simulation.py and Real.py call MPC.py on every control cycle.4.4.3 Mission Layer (Mission.py)Mission.py defines high-level mission behavior and generates the desired trajectory. It supports multiple mission types, including: Point-to-point moves. Waypoint sequences. Orbit-style or contour-following motion. Profile Following along an offset curve generated from a DXF outline.For the Profile Following mission used in Chapter 6, Mission.py loads the path derived from the imported DXF file, applies the specified standoff distance, applies the desired tracking speed, and produces the time-varying reference state \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ref}}. At every control step, both Simulation.py and Real.py query Mission.py to obtain the next target pose and (when needed) desired velocity. The MPC controller then attempts to track that reference target.4.4.4 Simulation Environment (Simulation.py)Simulation.py runs closed-loop missions in software. It operates two coupled loops: A physics loop that advances the satellite state using the rigid-body dynamics in Section 4.2 (including force and torque accumulation, Euler integration, and angle wrapping). A control loop that calls MPC.py at the same 60 ms interval used on the physical system.At each control interval, Simulation.py: Reads the current simulated state. Requests the current mission reference from Mission.py. Calls MPC.py to compute the optimal thruster command. Applies that thruster command to the simulated plant. Logs state reference, thruster commands, and solver timing.Simulation.py can run either in idealized mode or realistic mode (Section 4.3.2). It writes all data to csv file in a structured log format that is shared with the hardware controller. This makes it possible to generate the same plots and animations for both simulated and physical runs.4.4.5 Hardware Control Loop (Real.py and motive_data_server.py)Real.py is the real-time controller that runs the physical satellite using live sensor feedback. Its structure mirrors Simulation.py, but instead of advancing a simulated satellite, it sends commands to real thrusters and reads motion capture feedback.Each 60 ms control cycle, Real.py: Acquires the current satellite pose and velocity estimate from the external tracking system. A dedicated interface (motive_data_server.py) streams OptiTrack motion capture data into the controller. Requests the current reference from Mission.py. Passes the measured state and reference to MPC.py. Receives the 8-bit thruster command vector from MPC.py. Checks workspace limits, linear velocity limits, and angular rate limits for safety. Sends the validated thruster command to the solenoid valve controller over the hardware interface. Logs the full cycle (state, reference, command, solver timing) in the same format used by Simulation.py.Because both Real.py and Simulation.py call the same MPC.py with the same Config.py, the only fundamental difference between a simulated run and a hardware run is the system being controlled: simulated dynamics versus the real air-bearing system. This one-to-one symmetry is what enables the direct comparison in Chapter 6.4.4.6 Visualization and Data Products (Visualize.py and Logging_Config.py)After each run, the logged data is processed into plots and animations.Visualize.py reads the logged time histories and produces: Actual vs. commanded trajectory overlays. Position error and heading error versus time. Velocity magnitude versus time. Per-thruster duty cycle and total thruster usage. MPC solver time per control step. An animation (MP4) of the satellite's motion and commanded thruster firings.The plotting and animation pipeline is identical for simulation logs and hardware logs, because both are written in a common format. This is how the comparison figures in Chapter 6 (trajectory overlay, position error, angle error, velocity, thruster usage, and solver timing) were generated.Logging_Config.py standardizes timestamping and organizes output data into csv files. It ensures that state history, reference history, solver timing, and commanded thruster states are recorded every control cycle.4.4.7 Summary of Software FlowIn normal operation, Mission.py defines "what to do" by generating a target pose over time. MPC.py determines "how to do it" by selecting which thrusters should fire to track that target while satisfying constraints. Simulation.py or Real.py applies those thruster commands to the system. Simulation.py applies them to the virtual physics model while Real.py applies them to the physical solenoid valves. Config.py guarantees that both paths use consistent parameters, and Visualize.py transforms the resulting logs into plots and animations for analysis.This software structure allows missions to be rehearsed and tuned in simulation before committing hardware time. It also enables direct comparison between predicted behavior and observed behavior, as presented in Chapter 6.4.5 3D CAD Model in Autodesk Fusion 360In parallel with the simulation software, a detailed 3D CAD model of the platform was created in Autodesk Fusion 360. The digital twin predicts the dynamic response to commanded control inputs and supports controller design.4.6 Time and Resource SavingsThe combined digital twin and software architecture provided several practical benefits during development:Rapid controller tuning:MPC parameters such as horizon lengths, cost weights, and constraint limits were evaluated in simulation before hardware testing. Dozens of parameter combinations could be screened in software in the time it would take to set up and execute a single hardware run. Only configurations that performed well in simulation were advanced to physical testing.Mission planning:High-level mission types (including waypoint navigation, orbit-style motion, and Profile Following around an imported shape) were developed and debugged in software first. Poor strategies could be discarded early. Only approaches that demonstrated acceptable performance in simulation were promoted to hardware trials.Reduced wear on hardware:Each hardware test consumes compressed air and causes wear on mechanical components. This reduced risk before running closed-loop control on hardware.Overall, the digital twin and the supporting software stack allowed missions to be rehearsed, tuned, and analyzed in simulation and then executed on hardware using the same controller. The result is a one-to-one comparison between simulated performance and physical performance, which is quantified in Chapter 6.Chapter 5: Model Predictive Control Formulation5.1 IntroductionModel Predictive Control (MPC) serves as the core decision-making algorithm that computes optimal thruster firing sequences to navigate the satellite from its current state to the desired target state. This chapter presents the mathematical formulation of the MPC controller, explaining how the satellite dynamics, constraints, and objectives are programmed into an optimization problem that can be solved in real-time.The MPC implementation addresses several fundamental challenges in satellite control: the binary nature of the thruster actuation (on/off only), the need to minimize fuel consumption while achieving precise positioning, the requirement to respect velocity and position limits, and the necessity to compute solutions within strict time constraints (60 milliseconds per control cycle). The resulting formulation is a Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) that is solved at each control cycle using the Gurobi optimization solver.5.2 MPC Fundamentals5.2.1 Receding Horizon Control PrincipleModel Predictive Control operates on the principle of receding horizon optimization. At each control interval, the controller: Measures the current system state from sensors Predicts system behavior over a finite future horizon Optimizes a sequence of control actions over this prediction horizon Applies only the first control action from the optimal sequence Repeats the entire process at the next time step with updated measurementsThis receding horizon approach provides several advantages for satellite control. The continuous re-optimization using fresh state measurements provides robustness to disturbances, if an unexpected force disturbs the satellite, the next optimization cycle automatically compensates using the updated position measurement. The finite prediction horizon keeps the optimization problem computationally manageable while still providing sufficient lookahead to plan smooth maneuvers. Unlike classical controllers that handle constraints through workaround techniques such as saturation and anti-windup logic, MPC integrates constraints directly into the optimization, enabling more reliable and precise control near operational limits.5.2.2 Prediction and Control HorizonsThe MPC formulation uses two distinct time horizons that serve different purposes:Prediction Horizon (N): The number of future time steps over which the system behavior is predicted. For this implementation, N = 15 steps, corresponding to a prediction window of 0.9 seconds (15 steps ? 60 ms per step). This horizon length was selected to provide sufficient lookahead for smooth trajectory planning while maintaining computational efficiency. The prediction horizon enables the controller to anticipate future system evolution and account for longer-term consequences when computing current control actions. While longer prediction horizons enhance optimality by incorporating more future information into the optimization, they also increase computational demands. Conversely, shorter horizons can result in short-sighted decision-making and more frequent course corrections.Control Horizon (M): The number of future time steps for which control inputs are explicitly optimized. For this implementation, M = 12 < N = 15, meaning control actions are computed for the first 12 steps (0.72 seconds) of the prediction horizon, while control inputs for steps 13–15 are held constant at the value from step 12. This configuration provides a practical balance between computational efficiency and control performance. The full prediction horizon (N = 15) allows the optimization to account for long-term system dynamics and constraints, improving trajectory quality and stability. However, by limiting the control degrees of freedom to M = 12, the problem size and computational burden are reduced compared to the M = N case. This approach is particularly advantageous when the near-term control actions (within M) have the dominant influence on system performance, while still benefiting from extended predictive awareness for planning purposes.The discretization time step \Delta t=0.06seconds (60 milliseconds) defines the temporal resolution of both horizons. This time step matches the physical control update rate, the rate at which new thruster commands can be sent to the hardware. Selecting \Delta t requires balancing several factors: it must be fast enough to capture system dynamics and respond to disturbances yet slow enough that the MPC solver can find solutions within the allocated time. The 60 ms interval was determined through testing to provide good control performance while maintaining computational feasibility with the Gurobi solver. 5.3 System Dynamics Model5.3.1 State-Space RepresentationThe satellite system is represented using a discrete-time state-space model that describes how the state evolves from one time step to the next under the influence of control inputs. The linearized model provides an approximation to the true nonlinear dynamics that is sufficiently accurate over the short prediction horizon.5.5.2 Actuator ConstraintsThe binary nature of the solenoid valves is captured through integer constraints:,,u-k,i.?,0,1. for i=1,…,8,  ?k=0,…,M-1#,5.18..This constraint transforms the optimization into a Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP), which is computationally more demanding than a standard Linear Program but is necessary to accurately represent the on/off actuation without artificial relaxation to continuous control. The MILP solver (Gurobi) uses branch-and-bound algorithms to search the discrete solution space.5.5.3 State ConstraintsPhysical and safety limits constrain the allowable predicted states throughout the horizon.Position Bounds:,-3.0≤,p-x.,,p-y.≤3.0 [m]#,5.19..These bounds prevent the satellite from leaving the 6 m ? 6 m test floor and ensure the motion capture system maintains visibility of the reflective markers mounted on the satellite body.Velocity Limits:,-0.15≤,v-x.,,v-y.≤0.15 [m/s]#,20..,-0.349≤?≤0.349 [rad/s],±20°/s.#,5.21..These limits prevent excessive velocities that could cause hardware damage in the event of a collision or loss of control. The linear velocity limit of 0.15 m/s was selected as a conservative safety threshold, significantly below speeds that would damage the structure but high enough to enable reasonable maneuver times across the test area. The angular velocity limit prevents rapid spinning that could complicate state estimation.Angle Bounds:,-2π≤?≤2π [rad]#,5.22..The angle is bounded to \pm2\pi to accommodate full rotations while preventing numerical issues in the optimization. Angle wraparound is handled through preprocessing (described in Section 5.8.2) that adjusts the target angle to lie in the optimal neighborhood of the current angle, avoiding the "long way around" problem where the optimizer might command a 270° rotation instead of a ?90° rotation to reach the same final orientation. All constraints are expressed as linear inequalities, making them compatible with the MILP formulation and solvable by the Gurobi optimizer.5.6 Mixed-Integer Linear Programming FormulationThe complete MPC problem at each control cycle is formulated as a Mixed-Integer Linear Program. Gurobi uses a branch-and-bound algorithms with cutting planes to explore the discrete solution space created by the binary thruster variables.Key Solver Parameters:Several solver parameters are tuned specifically for real-time MPC applications where consistent timing is more critical than guaranteed optimality:TimeLimit (50 ms): A hard constraint on solve time (slightly less than the 60 ms control period to allow for overhead) ensures the MPC returns a solution before the next control cycle begins. If this limit is reached, Gurobi returns the best solution found so far. In practice, the solver almost always finds near-optimal solutions well before this deadline.MIPGap (1%): The relative optimality tolerance allows early termination when a solution is proven within 1% of the global optimum. This relaxation significantly improves solve speed with negligible impact on control performance, in practice, 99% optimal solutions perform indistinguishably from truly optimal solutions for this application.MIPFocus (1): Directs the solver to prioritize finding feasible solutions quickly rather than proving global optimality. For real-time control, a good feasible solution delivered on time is preferable to an optimal solution delivered too late.Heuristics (1% time): Limits time spent on heuristic solution generation to 1% of the time budget. While heuristics can accelerate solution finding for large problems, for small MPC problems the overhead can exceed the benefit.Cuts (Moderate): Enables moderate generation of cutting planes to tighten the linear programming relaxation. Aggressive cut generation improves bounds but increases per-iteration computational cost.Presolve (Aggressive): Enables aggressive presolve to simplify the problem before branch-and-bound begins. Presolve can reduce problem size through variable elimination and constraint tightening, which is particularly effective for structured MPC problems.Threads (1): Forces single-threaded execution. For small MPC problems, multi-threading overhead exceeds parallelization benefits, and single-threaded execution provides more predictable and consistent timing behavior.ImproveStartTime (60% of time limit): After spending 60% of the time limit improving the initial solution, the solver begins focusing more on proving optimality. This ensures good solutions are found quickly while still making progress toward optimality.These parameters were tuned through multiple tests to maximize the probability of finding high-quality solutions within the 50 ms deadline while maintaining consistent solver performance. The configuration prioritizes consistent real-time performance over absolute optimality.5.7.2 Warm Starting StrategyTo accelerate convergence, the MPC implementation uses warm starting by providing the solver with an initial guess based on the previous solution. Since the system evolves smoothly and optimal control decisions tend to remain consistent over short time intervals, the previous solution shifted forward in time provides an excellent starting point for the next optimization.At time step k+1, the warm start is constructed as follows: State trajectory initialization:,,x-j-init.=,x-j+1-prev. for j=0,1,…,N-1#,5.30..,,x-N-init.=,x-N-prev.#,5.31..Control sequence initialization:,,u-j-init.=,u-j+1-prev. for j=0,1,…,M-2#,5.32..,,u-M-1-init.=,u-M-1-prev.#,5.33..This warm start provides the solver with a feasible starting point that is typically close to the true optimum at the next time step, reducing the number of branch-and-bound iterations required and improving solve time consistency.5.7.3 Fallback Control StrategyDespite careful solver configuration, extreme scenarios can rise where the optimizer fails to find a feasible solution within the time limit. Potential causes include: Overly aggressive state constraints that conflict with dynamics Numerical issues in the MILP formulation Unexpected solver behaviorTo maintain system safety and prevent control freezing, a fallback proportional controller activates automatically when the MILP solver fails. The fallback logic examines the dominant error component and activates thrusters that directly oppose this error:,,u-i-fallback.=,,1-if thruster i reduces dominant error-0-otherwise..#,5.34..The fallback controller operates as follows: Identify the largest error component among position (p_x, p_y), angle (\theta), and their corresponding velocities  Select thrusters whose activation would reduce this dominant error Command those thrusters on for the current control cycleWhile suboptimal compared to MPC, this simple strategy ensures the satellite continues making progress toward the target rather than stalling in place. During all hardware testing, the fallback controller was never activated.5.8 Implementation Architecture5.8.1 Persistent Model ApproachThe MPC implementation uses a persistent optimization model that is constructed once at initialization and reused across all control cycles. Rather than rebuilding the entire MILP model from scratch every 60 milliseconds, only the numerical values of constraint right- hand sides and objective coefficients are updated with new data.Model Construction (once at startup): Create all decision variables (states, controls, switching indicators) Define the structure of all constraints (dynamics, bounds, switching) Specify the objective function form Configure solver parametersModel Update (every control cycle): Update initial condition: \mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{measured}}^{\mathrm{new}} Update dynamics matrices: A_k^{\mathrm{new}},B_k^{\mathrm{new}} from current linearization  Update target state: \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\mathrm{new}} Update adaptive weights: Q^{\mathrm{new}}(if in damping zone)  Update warm start: shift previous solution forwardThis approach eliminates the computing cost of repeated model construction, including variable creation, constraint generation, and memory allocation.5.8.2 Angle Wraparound HandlingA subtle but important implementation detail involves handling angle wraparound. Because angles repeat every 2\pi radians, a target angle of -170° is physically identical to +190°, even though their numerical values differ by a full rotation (360°). Without special handling, the optimizer might incorrectly command a complete unnecessary rotation instead of a small 20° adjustment. The implementation preprocesses the target angle before each optimization to ensure it lies within \pm\pi of the current angle: ,,?-ref-adjusted.=,?-current.+wrap,,?-ref.-,?-current..#,5.35..where the wrap function normalizes angles to the range [-\pi,+\pi]: ,wrap,?.=atan2,,sin-?.,,cos-?..#,5.36..This adjustment ensures the target angle lies within \pm180°of the current angle, guaranteeing the optimizer selects the shortest rotational path. This preprocessing occurs before every optimization solve, without it the controller might command the satellite to rotate 270° counterclockwise when a simple 90° clockwise rotation would achieve the same orientation. 5.9 Real-Time Computational PerformanceThe MPC controller was extensively tested to verify real-time feasibility. The simulation predicts this behavior within small margins. However, several limitations apply.Planar motion only. The tested system moves in-plane (p_x, p_y) and rotates about a vertical axis (\theta). Out-of-plane motion and full 6-DOF coupling are not exercised. The air-bearing support on a flat floor approximates planar microgravity, but orbital effects, gravity gradients, plume impingement, and cross-axis coupling are not represented. External state estimation. The hardware controller uses OptiTrack motion capture for position and orientation feedback. This provides high-rate, high-accuracy absolute pose information. An on-orbit system would not have access to this class of external sensing and would rely on onboard navigation with drift and latency. Closed-loop performance under degraded sensing is not evaluated here.Ground-based computation. The MPC problem is solved on a ground computer, and on/off thruster commands are sent wirelessly to the satellite at 16.7 Hz. Although the solver consistently remained under the 60 ms control deadline (average 4.61 ms, worst 32.10 ms), this does not yet prove that the exact same MILP can run on embedded flight-class hardware with stricter compute and memory limits. The observed timing margin suggests feasibility, but this has not been demonstrated onboard.Controlled environment. Tests were run on a smooth epoxy floor with regulated air supply and minimal external disturbance.Thruster and pneumatics modeling. Each thruster was individually characterized and its measured thrust magnitude was used in the controller. The eight thrusters differed in output by up to 13.2%. The controller successfully compensated for this within the mission, but long-term changes due to wear or center-of-mass shift were not studied.Mission duration. The mission analyzed here lasted about 196 seconds. Longer missions may introduce slow drift in center of mass, temperature effects, regulator pressure decay, or solver timing degradation under changing system voltage. Those effects were not evaluated.These limitations define the scope of what has been demonstrated. What has been shown is that mixed-integer MPC with binary thrusters can run in real time at a 60 ms control period for a multi-minute mission without missing deadlines, and that the digital twin can predict final position, final heading, integrated thruster usage, and solver timing with useful accuracy. What has not yet been demonstrated is fully onboard solve of the MPC on flight-like embedded hardware, closed-loop control using only onboard sensing (no external motion capture), full six-degree-of-freedom maneuvering in a realistic orbital environment, and robustness to significant parameter uncertainty over long-duration operation.These items define the immediate next steps: onboard solver integration, onboard state estimation, longer-duration trials, and extension of the controller and simulation to full 6-DOF.Chapter 7: Conclusions and Future Work7.1 Research SummaryThis thesis investigated whether a model predictive controller (MPC) commanding binary (on/off) thrusters can (1) control a planar free-floating satellite prototype in real time, and (2) be developed primarily in simulation and transferred to hardware without parameter tuning.Three integrated components were developed:Physical planar satellite prototype: A 290 mm ? 290 mm air-bearing satellite with eight independently actuated cold-gas thrusters, capable of translation in x, y, and rotation about the vertical axis. Mixed-integer MPC controller: A real-time controller solving a finite-horizon mixed-integer linear program (MILP) every 60 ms to produce optimal thruster firing patterns that track desired trajectories while respecting actuation, velocity, and safety limits.High-fidelity digital twin: A simulation environment using the same MPC implementation, mass properties, thruster locations, calibrated thrust magnitudes, and update rates as hardware. The twin includes realistic effects such as damping and communication delay, calibrated using physical data.The validation experiment executed the same mission in both simulation and hardware: an "inspection-style" profile-following path at 0.50 m standoff distance and 0.07 m/s tracking speed. The satellite followed curved segments, sharp corners, and straight lines while maintaining position and heading objectives over approximately 196 seconds.Results showed: MPC met real-time deadlines throughout the full mission Physical system tracked the commanded path with mean position error of 0.179 m and final position error of 0.024 m Final heading error of 0.51° Total thruster usage predicted by simulation matched hardware within 1.5% Trajectory shape in simulation and hardware was nearly identicalThese findings demonstrate the digital twin can be used for controller tuning, mission rehearsal, and performance prediction prior to hardware execution.7.2 Key Contributions7.2.1 Planar Satellite Testbed for Binary-Thruster MPCA fully integrated planar satellite was designed and built, including: Square 3-DOF air-bearing platform Eight individually addressable cold-gas thrusters Onboard embedded electronics for command execution External motion capture for state estimation Compressed air supply and multi-regulator pneumatic systemThe system behaves dynamically like a free-flyer in a plane: it exhibits inertia-dominated motion, relies entirely on impulsive actuation for control, and has limited actuation authority. This provides a repeatable, ground-based environment for validating satellite-like control problems.7.2.2 Real-Time Mixed-Integer Model Predictive Control with Binary ActuatorsAn MPC controller was implemented that directly reasons over eight binary thruster commands at each control step. The controller solves a MILP with a receding horizon of 0.9 s (prediction horizon N=15 steps at 60 ms each) and control horizon of 0.72 s (M=12). The optimization includes linearized system dynamics, actuator limits, velocity bounds, and a cost function penalizing position/heading error and total thruster usage. Hardware trial performance: Mean solve time: 4.61 ms Worst-case solve time: 32.10 ms No control cycle exceeded the 60 ms deadlineThis experimentally verifies that branch-and-bound MILP-based control of a thruster-actuated platform is feasible at 16.7 Hz.7.2.3 Thruster Characterization and Algorithmic CompensationEach thruster was measured in situ, with eight measured thrust magnitudes differing by up to ~13%. Instead of matching thrusters performance to enforce uniformity, the controller and simulation directly used each thruster's measured force.The MPC controlled the platform successfully despite this asymmetry, and simulation predicted total propellant usage within 1.5%, showing that algorithmic compensation for actuator non-uniformity is viable in practice.7.2.4 Mission-Level Digital Twin with Shared Control StackA digital twin was developed that runs the exact same MPC code, parameter set, and mission definition used on hardware. The twin includes damping terms, actuation timing delays, and measured physical parameters (mass, inertia, thruster geometry, thrust levels). The mission flown on hardware was rehearsed in the twin without changing the controller. After minor environment parameter tuning (e.g., effective drag), the simulation reproduced hardware behavior in trajectory shape, path timing, and final settling accuracy (centimeter-level final position, sub-degree final heading).This demonstrates the digital twin is quantitatively predictive for path tracking, fuel usage, and settling quality.7.2.5 Quantitative Simulation-to-Hardware Comparison MethodologyThe thesis introduces and applies a comparison framework between simulation and hardware using: Mean and maximum position error Mean and maximum heading error Final settling error in both position and heading Velocity profile over time Total thruster on-time (propellant usage proxy) Solver timing statisticsThis framework allows for direct comparison between simulation and hardware7.3 Performance SummaryControl Performance (hardware run): Mean position error during tracking: 0.179 m Final position error: 0.024 m Mean heading error during tracking: 12.72° Final heading error: 0.51° Mean velocity: 0.0630 m/s (commanded: 0.07 m/s) Maximum velocity stayed below 0.15 m/s safety limit Mission length: ~12.4 m path in 196.16 sComputational Performance: Control update rate: 16.7 Hz (60 ms period) Mean MILP solve time: 4.61 ms Maximum MILP solve time: 32.10 ms Missed deadlines: 0Simulation vs Hardware Agreement: Final position error: 0.024 m (hardware) (hardware) vs 0.0642 m/s (simulation), 1.9% difference Mean heading error difference: ~5.5% Mean position error difference: ~21%These results confirm the MPC can operate in real time with discrete on/off actuators and that the digital twin predicts mission-level behavior closely enough to be operationally useful for controller tuning and mission rehearsal.7.4 Limitations and Challenges7.4.1 Tracking Accuracy and Intentional TradeoffsDuring most of the path, position error was 0.1–0.3 m and heading error was typically 10–20°. This is suitable for "stand off and survey" missions but not precision docking or capture.This behavior is partly intentional. The MPC cost function penalizes both tracking error and thruster usage. With only binary thrusters (no partial thrust levels), perfect instantaneous correction would require constant aggressive firing, causing inefficiency, overshoot, and chatter. The controller balances spending fuel to reduce error quickly versus accepting moderate error to save fuel and reduce high-frequency switching.The satellite "lags and corrects" through high-curvature segments but settles accurately at the end. Final position and heading errors are excellent, though mid-trajectory errors are larger.7.4.2 Planar Testbed ScopeThe platform is restricted to planar motion: x, y, and yaw. Out-of-plane translation, roll/pitch coupling, gravity gradients and full 6-DOF coupling are not exercised. This means:  No demonstration of full 6-DOF attitude stabilization No coordinated 3D translational and rotational maneuvers No control in presence of orbital perturbations or environmental torquesThe planar regime captures impulsive actuation, inertia-dominated drift, and discrete-thruster regulation of translation and heading, but it is not a full six-degree-of-freedom orbital environment.7.4.3 Environmental and Actuation Non-IdealitiesHardware includes several real-world effects difficult to model perfectly: Solenoid valves have opening/closing delay and pressure-dependent thrust buildup Air bearings produce very low, but nonzero drag Center of mass is slightly offset (~millimeters) from geometric center, so "pure translation" firings introduce torque The OptiTrack/Motive system introduces minor data noise and transmission latency. As compressed air is consumed during the mission and not retained onboard, the center of mass shifts slightly over time.7.4.4 External State Estimation and Offboard ComputationThe current system relies on: OptiTrack motion capture at 240 Hz for precise pose feedback Ground station computer to solve MPC MILP every 60 ms Wireless transmission of resulting 8-bit thruster commandThis is acceptable for lab demonstration but not flight-like. Satellite cannot assume access to external tracking or offboard solvers. Onboard sensing and onboard solve are important for autonomy.7.5 Future Work7.5.1 Onboard MPC ExecutionCurrently, optimization is solved on a ground computer with only the final thruster command sent wirelessly. The next step is closing that loop onboard.Placing an embedded compute module (e.g., small single-board computer) on the satellite would enable: Onboard state estimation Onboard MILP solve Direct actuation Only high-level supervision from groundThis would remove the wireless link from the control loop and more closely emulate a flight system.7.5.2 Autonomous State Estimation Without Motion CaptureThe platform includes onboard sensors (cameras and ultrasonic distance sensors,) not used for control in this work.Future work should replace external motion capture with: Onboard vision Range sensing (ultrasonic/LiDAR) Inertial sensing (IMU for angular rate and acceleration)This step is important for making the platform a true autonomy testbed rather than a supervised lab demonstrator.7.5.3 Geometry and Thruster Layout StudiesThe current satellite is square with eight thrusters (two per side), chosen to provide redundancy and controllability while keeping optimization feasible.The simulation environment can now explore alternate geometries without rebuilding hardware: Triangular body with six thrusters Hexagonal or octagonal bodies with higher actuation redundancy Asymmetric thruster placement to bias torque authority Different moment arms for yaw controlFor each geometry, evaluate tracking accuracy, fuel usage, and solver timing. This informs future designs, enabling "co-design" where mechanical layout and controller capability are optimized together.7.5.4 Automated Digital Twin Calibration and 6-DOF ExtensionCurrently, matching simulation to hardware required manual adjustment of damping and disturbance terms. A logical next step is automating calibration using system identification: fit simulation parameters so simulated trajectories match flight data in a least-squares sense. This would tighten prediction accuracy and reduce tuning effort.Extending the testbed to six degrees of freedom is an obvious long-term goal, requiring a full microgravity free-flyer. Such a system would enable testing full 6-DOF MPC with binary thrusters, onboard solve, and onboard estimation a direct step toward on-orbit autonomous inspection and servicing satellite.7.6 Closing RemarksThis work demonstrates that: Mixed-integer MPC with binary thrusters can run in real time on a physical platform for multi-minute missions without missing MPC solver deadlines. A digital twin using measured parameters can predict mission-level performance (trajectory shape, final settling accuracy, total thruster usage) with useful accuracy. Algorithmic compensation for hardware imperfection is a viable strategy for dealing with actuator non-uniformity or in case of hardware failure.This work successfully demonstrates that mixed-integer Model Predictive Control (MPC) commanding binary thrusters can operate in real time on a physical platform, meeting all solver deadlines during multi-minute mission profiles. Furthermore, the research validates that a digital twin, developed using measured hardware parameters, can predict mission-level performance-including trajectory shape, final settling accuracy, and total thruster usage-with useful accuracy. It also confirms that algorithmic compensation is a viable strategy for managing actuator non-uniformity and potential hardware failures, shifting focus from hardware perfection to intelligent control.Collectively, these results provide experimental validation that advanced optimal control methods can be deployed on prototype hardware using commercially available components, and crucially, that simulation-based development reliably predicts this hardware behavior.
